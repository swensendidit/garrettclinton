For the past year here at Stanford, I have been researching how first-generation wealth creators build trust with the people who ultimately end up advising them.

My interest here began early in my career. The multigenerational families I worked with then had spent decades building endowment-style structure around themselves: best-in-class liquidity and tax strategy, careful trust and estate planning, clear decisions about which teams to keep in-house or outsource, and a disciplined investment process to tie it all together.

Today, most first-generation families are being asked to make those same decisions in a matter of months, and often all at once.

After years spent creating and building, I've noticed these families rarely get the time to shift toward preserving it. So, over the last year, I have spent time with families, principals, CIOs, and advisors at very different moments, from the months before a company goes public to the years after a major athletic contract or a pivotal inheritance. What began as a personal curiosity about the right mode of investing for first-generation wealth creators has evolved into a study of the behavioral incentives behind the different models that serve families as they begin to take on the work of an institution.

My view

My research so far suggests that comparing fees alone tells families surprisingly little about the value they’re receiving or the incentives shaping their advice. I believe there are three core questions beyond fees that are crucial to ask when selecting an advisor:

Valuing the moment

FoundersYou have probably spent an entire market cycle making consequential decisions for your business, all while building the muscle of being responsible for others.
Early employeesYou committed to a company before most people believed in it and stayed through the stretches.
AthletesYou built an empire on discipline and performance, in an unbelievably short amount of time.

What is the best way to design the next chapter of your family's legacy and assets, while honoring who you are and what you most care about?

The choice between building something of your own and handing the work to an outside firm tends to come all at once, usually before anyone in your family has had the chance to understand what each option asks of you. As a result, it is easy to settle into a wealth management model by default, long before you would have chosen one with the same intention you have given to your career.

The families who fare best are those who understand what the business model rewards relative to what the family values.

Incentives and the architecture of trust

Across my conversations with families and the advisors, agents, managers, and teams around them, the first priorities raised are rarely returns or fees. Trust, privacy, next-generation education and, in some cases, greater autonomy matter far more.

Each of those comes down to who a family trusts with its affairs, and how that relationship is paid.

Do I like this person, and can they do the job?

These are the two questions families ask most often when choosing an outside partner for their investments. The strongest external partners, however, hold up to three pillars: character, competence, and behavioral incentives the family understands. Every way of paying an advisor shapes what they are inclined to recommend, and understanding that early is a way of respecting both the advisor and the family.

How the advisor is paidWhat it means for you
  1. Asset feesSome advisors are paid a percentage of the money they manage for you. Their pay is tied to the amount of assets you keep with them, so they may be less eager when you want to move money out to pay down debt or start a business.
  2. ProductsSome advisors are paid when you buy an investment product, such as a fund or an insurance policy. If their company also makes its own funds, it earns a fee on those funds on top of the asset or flat fee you may already pay.
  3. Cash and lendingSome advisors work for a bank that also earns money from your cash and your loans. For example, the bank might pay you 1% on cash sitting in your account while earning 4% on it, and it collects interest when you borrow against your investments. Neither shows up in the advisor’s fee, but both give the bank a reason to keep your cash and to suggest borrowing.
  4. CarrySome investors are paid a share of the profit when an investment is sold at a gain. They share in the wins but not the losses, which can encourage bigger, riskier bets than your situation calls for.
  5. Flat feesSome advisors charge a set annual fee, no matter how much you have or how it is invested. That removes most of these conflicts, but every extra request costs the advisor time without adding pay. As your needs grow, with a new trust, a business sale, or a family member to support, the fee often has to be revisited so that pay keeps pace with the work, and until it is, the advisor has a reason to do less.

None of this makes anyone dishonest, and many of the best advisors work well within exactly these structures. My research has taught me that families are comfortable paying for good advice, as long as they understand how it is paid for. Trust tends to break when they later learn the incentives behind their advisor were not aligned with the long-term goals of their family.

Ask any advisor you are considering

“How did your company start?”

Most advisors work within a larger company, and how that company started can tell you a lot about the frame of thinking its advisors bring to your family. The business it was first built to run, and the clients who first paid it, still show up in what it does well, whom it hires, and what it suggests first. Most advisors work for a company that traces back to one of four starting points, and each tends to show up as one of the options in this piece.

Read down the second column. Three of the four began by earning from something sold alongside the advice: a product, a loan, or a plan. The fourth began by being paid to manage the money itself, so its pay is tied to how that money grows over time. Every company adds services over time, and most have added plenty, though some are far easier to add than others, as the last column shows. Ask an advisor how their company started, and how it makes most of its money today, and you learn a lot about what they will suggest.

The models that serve families

Robo-advisors, private wealth advisors, private banks, independent multi-family offices, and single-family offices each have a long record of serving certain families well, and which one fits depends most on the moment a family is in and what it wants for the people it is planning for.

Looking ahead

Across all five, AI is lowering the cost of work that once required a large team. That is shifting where each option makes sense, and it is likely to change these ranges over the next few years.
